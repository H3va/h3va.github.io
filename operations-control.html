AI-Assisted Operations Control & Exception Reporting

Excel dashboard · Rule-based validation · AI prompting · Human review · Process improvement

Fictional-bank prototypeOctober 2026No live systems or customer records
01 / THE PROBLEM

Project objective

How can teams identify incomplete or conflicting operational records without treating AI-generated decisions as authorized actions?

I designed an Excel-based prototype for a fictional bank, combining explainable data-quality rules, controlled AI analysis, human-review requirements and a small exploratory efficiency benchmark.

03 / METHOD

How the workflow works

01Validate inputs

Excel checks for missing case identifiers, duplicate case IDs and invalid review dates.

02Assess evidence

Standardized AI prompts classify exceptions as Yes, No or Unknown and identify missing evidence.

03Escalate ambiguity

Potential exceptions and unresolved policy questions remain subject to human review; no automatic approvals or record modifications.

04Document outcomes

Stress-test logs, a data dictionary and an adjudication register preserve the reasoning and open questions.

05 / CONTROLS

Reliability, ambiguity & human oversight

What was tested

  • Potentially duplicate vendor references
  • Conflicting status evidence
  • Prompt injection in operational comments
  • Missing timing and verification requirements

What remains unresolved

Four initial stress tests were logged. Two classification assessments passed their case-level checks; two remain pending adjudication because the supplied business definitions were incomplete.

All four logged safety/groundedness checks were marked Pass within the limited scenarios; this is not a security certification.

Scope and limitations

This is an independent educational prototype for a fictional financial institution. All records are synthetic. Benchmark measurements do not establish production-level accuracy, reliability, or realized savings. The scenario-based labour calculation is gross and excludes unverified implementation, AI/API and rework costs. No bank policy, employee data, or real-world approval is represented here.

06 / BUILD JOURNAL

How the project evolved

I started with a simple question about processing time, then realized that testing reliability and retaining human judgment matter just as much as speed. This is a learning and development log—not a claim that a bank adopted the system.

PHASE 01 · MODELING THE WORKFLOW

From exception records to an Excel dashboard

I organized 100 synthetic operations records and built an executive view of flagged cases and illustrative cost scenarios. The first lesson was that a dashboard can summarize a workflow without proving that a proposed efficiency gain is real.

Question I am still asking

When does a cost estimate become useful evidence? Only after its time assumptions, review steps and excluded expenses have been tested.

See the actual dashboard ↗
PHASE 02 · HUMAN REVIEW

Adding a review queue and prompt structure

I expanded the workbook to organize exception review and document prompts more consistently. Rather than treating an AI response as permission to act, the workflow retains uncertain decisions for human review.

Design principle

An AI-generated recommendation is not the same thing as an approved operational decision.

PHASE 03 · SMALL-SAMPLE EVALUATION

Checking classifications, time and control risks

I added a batch-evaluation framework, validation checks, exploratory time benchmarks and stress-test records. In two scenario pairs, AI-assisted processing was faster, but the sample is too small to generalize and the labor figures exclude deployment and rework.

What changed in my thinking

A faster answer is useful only if its evidence, uncertainty and escalation path are clear. In the next iteration I would prioritize independently adjudicated labels and a larger benchmark.

Model access & evidence

The screenshots above are from the project workbook. The editable multi-phase Excel file is being prepared for public release after a final review of formulas, notes and source attribution. Until then, the page distinguishes actual small-sample observations from illustrative projections.

07 / REFLECTION

What I learned

Better automation is not just about faster decisions. It also requires clear field definitions, trustworthy evidence, auditability, and a deliberate path for human judgment.

The next iteration would prioritize a larger controlled benchmark, independent ground-truth adjudication and full-cost measurement before evaluating production suitability.
